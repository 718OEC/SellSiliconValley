monitoring The Decade of Volatility

The 2000s were defined by two massive events: The Dot-Com Bust (2001) and the Great Recession (2008). Despite these shocks, prices in 2009 ($516k) finished higher than they started in 2000 ($464k), proving the long-term resilience of Silicon Valley real estate.

home Houses: The Bubble

Single-family homes saw the most dramatic swing. From a steady climb to $894k in 2007, prices crashed to $435k in early 2009—a buying opportunity we haven't seen since.

apartment Condos: The Affordable Entry

Condos followed a similar trajectory but offered an entry point. Peaking around $450k in 2006, they dipped below $250k during the recession, allowing many first-time buyers to enter the market.

timer Days on Market: The Slowdown

In the heat of the bubble (2005), homes sold in less than 10 days. During the recession (2008), inventory sat for 60+ days, a stark reminder of how quickly leverage shifts from sellers to buyers.

bar_chart Sales Volume: The Freeze

Sales volume tells the story of confidence. Volume plummeted in 2001 (Dot-Com) and again in 2007 (Subprime Crisis). However, the recovery in 2009 showed volume spiking as investors rushed in to buy at the bottom.

search Why It's Always a Good Time to Buy

History Rhymes: The AI Parallel

Today's AI boom feels eerily similar to the Dot-Com surge of 1999/2000. Just like then, we are seeing immense wealth creation. But even when the Dot-Com bubble burst in 2001, home prices merely paused—they didn't crash until the unrelated subprime crisis years later.

The Lesson: Tech corrections don't always equal housing crashes. Silicon Valley housing is resilient because land is finite.

tips_and_updates Strategy Corner (Lessons from history)

For Buyers

Fear is Opportunity

The best deals in this decade were found when headlines were terrifying (2001 & 2009). When the market freezes, you have negotiating power.

For Sellers

Competing with Banks

In the 2008 recession, traditional sellers weren't just competing with neighbors—they were fighting against REOs (Bank Owned Properties) and Short Sales priced to liquidate. Pricing ahead of the market was the only survival strategy.

history_edu A Retrospective: What Happened?

Timeline
2000

The Dot-Com Peak

The NASDAQ hit its all-time high in March 2000, fueling immense wealth and home buying in the Valley.

2001

The Tech Bust

The tech bubble burst. Stock portfolios vanished, and home prices took a hit as the local economy contracted.

2005

The Housing Bubble

Easy credit and loose lending standards fueled a massive run-up in prices, creating a speculative bubble completely detached from income fundamentals.

2008

The Great Recession

The global financial crisis froze the market. Foreclosures (REOs) spiked, and prices bottomed out in early 2009.

The Aftermath

The New Floor

The 2009 bottom established a floor ($500k) that we would never see again. The pain of 2009 cleared the deck for the massive 2012-2015 recovery, driven by the second tech boom (Social/Mobile).

Today's "floor" is nearly 4x higher, reminding us that in Silicon Valley, the long-term trend is always up.
