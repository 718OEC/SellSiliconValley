Asset Allocation: The Showdown

Single Family (SFH)

The Appreciation Play

Houses are valued based on comparable sales (comps) driven by emotional home-buyers, making them the ultimate vehicle for aggressive, long-term equity growth in Silicon Valley.

Upside: Maximum historic appreciation, highly liquid (easier to sell), lower tenant turnover, tenants generally maintain the property better.

Downside: Lower monthly cash flow, 100% vacancy risk when empty (if the one tenant leaves, income drops to $0).

Multi-Family (MFH)

The Cash Flow Engine

Duplexes, triplexes, and apartments are primarily valued based on the income they generate (NOI), making them highly insulated from emotional market swings.

Upside: Superior monthly cash flow, economies of scale (one roof, multiple incomes), mitigated vacancy risk (one unit empty still leaves others paying).

Downside: Slower overall appreciation, higher maintenance costs, increased tenant management/turnover.

Capital Strategy

Financing

Using Leverage

Purchasing an asset using borrowed capital (a mortgage). By putting only 20% down, you control 100% of the asset's appreciation. If a $1M property goes up 10%, you made $100k on a $200k investment—a 50% Return on Equity. However, leverage also amplifies risk during market downturns and requires managing monthly debt service.

Liquidity

Cash-Only Investing

Buying a property outright without a loan. This eliminates monthly mortgage payments, creating immediate, heavy monthly cash flow and making the investment practically recession-proof. The trade-off is "opportunity cost"—tying up $1M in one house limits your ability to spread that capital across multiple appreciating assets.

The Investor's Glossary

The technical language of Silicon Valley real estate capital.

DCF (Discounted Cash Flow)

A valuation method used to estimate the value of an investment based on its expected future cash flows, adjusted for the "time value of money" (because $100 today is worth more than $100 next year).

Cap Rate

The unleveraged rate of return on a property. Calculated by dividing the Net Operating Income (NOI) by the current market value. A 5% Cap Rate means you'd earn a 5% annual return if you bought it in all cash.

NOI (Net Operating Income)

Total gross income from the property minus all operating expenses (taxes, insurance, maintenance). Crucially, NOI does not include your mortgage payment or capital expenditures.

Cash-on-Cash Return

The pre-tax cash income earned divided by the total cash actually invested. If you put $200k down and the property nets $10k a year after the mortgage is paid, your CoC return is 5%.

1031 Exchange

A powerful tax loophole. It allows an investor to sell a property and defer paying all capital gains taxes if they reinvest the proceeds into a new "like-kind" property of equal or greater value.

LTV (Loan-to-Value)

The ratio of a loan to the value of an asset purchased. A $800k mortgage on a $1M home is an 80% LTV. Lenders use this to assess risk; lower LTV means better rates.
